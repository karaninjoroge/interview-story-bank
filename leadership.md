## Story 1: Leading the Hospital Queue Research Process

**Question This Answers:** "Tell me about a time you led a project or initiative."
**Competency:** Leadership, Ownership
**Context:** Hospital Queue Management PM case study

---

**Situation:**
I was designing a product to address long wait times in Kenyan public hospitals. The challenge was that I had a strong initial hypothesis — that the problem was inefficiency — but no evidence to confirm or challenge it. I needed to design and run a research process from scratch with no team, no budget, and no prior PM experience conducting user interviews.

**Task:**
My responsibility was to move from hypothesis to validated problem definition. I needed to design a research plan, identify and recruit participants across two user groups (patients and clinical staff), conduct interviews, synthesize findings, and translate them into product direction.

**Action:**
I started by writing out every assumption I was making explicitly — before talking to anyone. I listed eight assumptions about the problem, the user, and the solution. Then I designed interview questions specifically to test those assumptions rather than confirm them.

I recruited six participants across two groups: patients who had visited public outpatient departments recently, and clinical staff at Level 3–5 facilities. For each interview, I used a semi-structured format — a core question set with room to follow threads that emerged.

The most important decision I made was to schedule the synthesis before I had finished all interviews. This forced me to identify patterns early rather than drowning in raw data at the end.

**Result:**
The research invalidated three of my eight assumptions. Most significantly: the problem was not inefficiency — it was uncertainty. Patients tolerate long waits when they know their position. They abandon when they do not. This finding completely changed the product direction, from throughput optimization to information delivery. The PRD I wrote is built on this insight, not the one I started with.

**PM Skills This Demonstrates:**
Independent initiative · Research design · Assumption mapping · Discovery discipline · Intellectual honesty

## Story 2: Making a Prioritization Decision Under Constraint

**Question This Answers:** "Tell me about a time you had to make a difficult decision with incomplete information."
**Competency:** Decision-making, Prioritization, Leadership
**Context:** Hospital Queue Management MVP scoping

---

**Situation:**
When scoping the MVP for the hospital queue platform, I had to make a prioritization decision between two features that both had strong research support: a patient-facing SMS update system and a staff-facing real-time dashboard. Both were clearly needed. Building both in v1 would delay launch significantly.

**Task:**
I needed to decide which to prioritize, document the reasoning, and defend the choice against the alternative.

**Action:**
I used RICE scoring to structure the decision, but the numbers alone did not resolve it. The patient SMS system scored higher on reach. The staff dashboard scored lower on reach but was qualitatively different — it was an adoption prerequisite. Without nurse buy-in and a tool that solved their core problem, the patient-facing system would have no operational context to run within.

I wrote a prioritization document that framed this explicitly: the staff dashboard is not lower priority. It is a dependency. Without it, the patient system fails regardless of how well it is built. Both needed to be in MVP, but the dashboard needed to be built first.

**Result:**
Both features entered the MVP scope, with the staff dashboard as the development sequence anchor. This prevented a scenario where patient features shipped without the operational infrastructure to support them — a classic mistake in two-sided product launches.

**PM Skills This Demonstrates:**
Structured prioritization · Systems thinking · Decision documentation · Stakeholder perspective-taking
